# Presentation Strategy

Use during `DISTILL` and `NARRATE`.

The deck is a sequence of cognitive moves, not a container for source material.

## Central promise

Write one sentence:

> By the end, this audience will understand, remember, decide, or be able to do ______.

Every slide must support that promise.

If the source suggests several competing promises, select one primary promise and move the rest to appendix material or a separate presentation.

## Distillation ledger

Maintain an internal ledger:

| Source item | Importance | Audience value | Evidence status | Slide candidate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Concept, claim, example, relationship, or term | essential / supporting / optional / appendix / excluded | why it matters | explicit / derived / uncertain | role or omission |

Do not include an item only because it occupies many pages in the source.

## Importance test

A source item is essential when removing it would cause at least one of:

- the central promise becomes false
- the audience cannot follow the mechanism
- the conclusion lacks support
- a dangerous misunderstanding becomes likely
- a required action becomes impossible
- the narrative loses a necessary transition

Supporting content earns space when it:

- proves
- clarifies
- distinguishes
- illustrates
- makes memorable
- prevents a likely misconception

## Narrative tension

Strong presentations usually contain a tension:

- expectation versus reality
- problem versus constraint
- old model versus new model
- symptom versus mechanism
- risk versus control
- complexity versus usable model
- present state versus desired state
- choice versus consequence

Do not manufacture conflict absent from the source. Reveal the real tension already present.

## Narrative arcs

Choose or combine a clear arc.

### Explain

`Why it matters → Core model → Parts → Interaction → Implication`

### Persuade

`Current reality → Cost or opportunity → Evidence → Proposed response → Decision`

### Teach

`Prior knowledge → New concept → Worked example → Practice or application → Synthesis`

### Technical briefing

`Context → Architecture/model → Critical flows → Constraints/risks → Next actions`

### Incident or chronology

`Initial state → Trigger → Sequence → Consequence → Lessons`

### Worldbuilding or RPG

`Premise → Actors → Forces/relationships → Rules/conflict → Play possibilities`

### Scientific

`Question → Model → Evidence/process → Limits → Application`

The arc should be describable in one sentence.

## Opening

The opening must establish relevance and direction.

Possible opening jobs:

- present the central question
- expose a misconception
- show the consequence
- reveal the system at a glance
- place the audience inside a scenario
- state the practical outcome

Avoid empty title slides unless the setting requires one.

## Middle

The middle must earn complexity gradually.

Use:

- progressive disclosure
- repeated visual anchors
- concrete examples
- comparison
- cause and effect
- one mechanism per slide where possible
- summaries after dense sections

Do not introduce several new visual languages and several new concepts on the same slide.

## Conclusion

The conclusion must resolve the opening promise.

It may:

- synthesize the model
- show the final decision
- reveal a complete system
- state the operating principles
- give the next action
- return to the opening scenario with changed understanding

Do not end with a generic `Questions?` slide as the only conclusion. A question invitation may follow a real synthesis.

## Slide rhythm

Vary cognitive load intentionally.

A healthy rhythm may alternate:

- orientation
- dense explanation
- simple statement
- example
- diagram
- reflection
- synthesis

Do not make every slide equally dense.

After a complex diagram, provide a simpler interpretation or application slide when the audience needs it.

## Keywords

A keyword belongs on the slide when it:

- names a core concept
- anchors memory
- distinguishes alternatives
- represents a repeated semantic category
- is required vocabulary

Use a keyword consistently. Do not create synonyms for visual variety.

## Evidence

Show evidence proportionally to the objective.

For explanatory or educational decks:

- include the evidence needed to trust the model
- move full citations and methodological detail to notes or appendix

For persuasive or decision decks:

- make decisive evidence visible
- distinguish evidence from recommendation
- disclose uncertainty

Never fabricate statistics or transform an approximate claim into a precise number.

## Appendix strategy

Move content to appendix when it is:

- accurate and useful
- likely to answer specialist questions
- too detailed for the narrative
- a supporting table
- source provenance
- full terminology
- edge cases
- alternate architectures
- extended examples

Appendix slides still require clear titles and usable design.

## Duration planning

Use these default speaking estimates:

- orientation or statement slide: 30–60 seconds
- ordinary explanation slide: 60–120 seconds
- dense diagram or comparison: 2–4 minutes
- worked example or exercise: 3–8 minutes
- transition or summary: 30–90 seconds

Do not infer duration solely from slide count.

When the requested duration is fixed, adjust content rather than accelerating beyond comprehension.

## Presentation quality audit

Before storyboarding, verify:

- one central promise exists
- one real tension or organizing question exists
- the opening earns attention
- the middle increases understanding
- the conclusion resolves the promise
- each source item has a reason to appear
- appendix content does not interrupt the story
- the pace matches audience and time
